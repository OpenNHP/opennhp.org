OpenNHP vs. Shirudo: Hide the Door, or Shield the Room?

Two open-source eBPF defenses for the AI vulnerability era, and why you may want both.

NHP
The OpenNHP Team
November 27, 2026 Comparison eBPF AI Security
AI finds vulnerabilities faster than teams can patch them. OpenNHP and Shirudo are two different answers to the same gap.
Same Problem, Two Answers

On November 23, 2026, OpenAI and Cisco introduced Shirudo, an open-source runtime that loads signed eBPF "shields" into running Linux systems to block an exploit while a patch rolls out. It's a welcome project, and it starts from the same premise as OpenNHP: in the AI era, the gap between "vulnerability found" and "every system patched" is where attacks happen.

The two projects close that gap at different points. OpenNHP hides the service, so unauthorized traffic never reaches it. Shirudo shields the running system, so a specific exploit fails even when the traffic arrives.

Where Each One Acts
OpenNHP stops the attack before connection. Shirudo stops a known exploit after the request has reached the host.

The Key Difference: Before or After Contact
OpenNHP: deny the connection
A server with no open ports can't be scanned, fingerprinted or exploited by someone who hasn't authenticated. That protection doesn't depend on knowing the bug, so it also covers zero-days nobody has found yet.
Shirudo: neutralize the exploit
A shield blocks the specific code path a vulnerability uses, without a reboot. It works on services that must stay public, and it can stop an attacker who already has access, but each shield covers one known issue.

Shirudo's own documentation is clear about its limits: a shield's signature proves who shipped it, not that it's a correct mitigation, and it doesn't replace kernel updates. OpenNHP has limits too: it controls reachability, not what an authorized user does once connected. Neither replaces patching.

Which One Should You Use?
✓ Start with OpenNHP when…
  • The service has a known set of users: admin panels, internal apps, APIs, agent tools, staging
  • You want protection that doesn't wait for each new CVE
  • You run mixed platforms, not only patched Linux kernels
Add Shirudo when…
  • The service must stay public to anonymous visitors
  • A critical kernel or runtime bug can't be patched yet
  • You need a TPM-backed record of what protection is loaded

They work best together. OpenNHP shrinks the set of people who can reach a service from "the whole Internet" to "the users you approved". Shirudo then guards that service against a known exploit, even from inside that approved set. Hide the door, then shield the room.
